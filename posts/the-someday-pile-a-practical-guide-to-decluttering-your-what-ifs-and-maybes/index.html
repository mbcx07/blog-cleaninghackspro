The 'Someday' Pile: A Practical Guide to Decluttering Your 'What Ifs' and 'Maybes'

2026-06-24 · Decluttering · Tags: cleaninghackspro, decluttering, The

We all have that corner of the closet, that drawer in the kitchen, or that box in the garage where the 'someday' items live. The vintage dress you'll wear to a themed party (someday). The bread maker you'll use when you finally start baking (someday). The stack of craft supplies for a project you've been meaning to try (someday). These items aren't junk—they're full of potential, hope, and a little bit of guilt. But here's the hard truth: 'someday' is not a day on the calendar. It's a vague, ever-receding horizon that keeps your home cluttered and your mind occupied. As a professional cleaner and decluttering coach, I've seen countless homes weighed down by these aspirational possessions. The good news? You can break free from the 'someday' pile without sacrificing your dreams. This guide will show you how to declutter your 'what ifs' and 'maybes' with compassion, clarity, and a practical system that actually works.

Understanding the 'Someday' Pile: Why We Keep Things We Don't Use

The 'someday' pile is not a sign of laziness or disorganization—it's a reflection of our hopes, fears, and identity. We keep these items because they represent a version of ourselves we want to become. The exercise bike promises a fitter you. The French cookbook whispers of gourmet dinners. The box of art supplies holds the key to your creative side. Letting go feels like giving up on that potential, and that's genuinely painful.

But there's also a practical side: we're afraid of regret. What if you need that item next week? What if you can't afford to replace it? What if you're throwing away something valuable? This fear is amplified by the sunk cost fallacy—you spent money on it, so you feel you must keep it to justify the expense. The result? A home filled with 'someday' items that never see the light of day, while the space you actually live in becomes cramped and stressful.

Recognize that the 'someday' pile is a coping mechanism. It's a way to hold onto possibilities without having to commit to them. But the cost of that safety net is higher than you think.

The Hidden Cost of 'Someday': How Clutter Drains Your Time, Energy, and Wallet

Every item you keep 'just in case' takes up physical space, but it also takes up mental space. Studies show that visual clutter increases cortisol levels and reduces focus. When you walk past that pile of 'someday' items, your brain subconsciously registers it as unfinished business. It's a constant reminder of what you're not doing, which can lead to feelings of guilt and inadequacy.

Financially, the 'someday' pile is a money pit. You've already spent money on these items, but you're also spending money on storage—whether that's a larger apartment, a storage unit, or simply the cost of organizing bins and shelves. And let's not forget the opportunity cost: the money tied up in unused items could be invested in experiences or things you actually use. A 2019 study found that the average American home has over 300,000 items, and many of them are 'someday' items. That's a lot of wasted potential.

Time is another casualty. You spend time cleaning around these items, moving them when you need to access something else, and searching for things that are buried under the 'someday' pile. The clutter also makes it harder to find the things you do use, leading to frustration and wasted minutes every day. When you add it up, the 'someday' pile is stealing hours from your life each week.

The 'Someday' Audit: A Compassionate Questioning Process

To tackle your 'someday' pile, you need a systematic approach that respects your emotions while cutting through the excuses. I call this the 'Someday' Audit. It's a series of questions you ask yourself for each item in your 'someday' pile. The goal is not to force yourself to throw everything away, but to make conscious decisions based on your current reality, not a hypothetical future.

Here's how it works: take one item at a time and ask these questions:

  • Have I used this in the past 12 months? If the answer is no, it's a strong candidate for removal. But don't stop there—ask why you haven't used it.
  • Do I have a specific plan to use it in the next 6 months? 'Someday' is not a plan. A plan includes a date, a project, or a concrete event. If you can't name the occasion, it's time to let go.
  • Is this item replaceable? If it's a common item that you could easily buy again, keeping it 'just in case' is unnecessary. If it's truly irreplaceable (like a family heirloom), that's a different category—but most 'someday' items are not.
  • Does this item bring me joy or guilt? Be honest. If the item makes you feel guilty every time you see it, it's not serving you. Joy is a better indicator of value than potential.
  • What is the worst-case scenario if I let it go? Often, the worst case is that you'll need to buy a replacement, which is usually affordable and easy. The best case is that you free up space and mental energy.

Go through your 'someday' pile with these questions. You'll find that many items fail the test. For those that pass—meaning you have a concrete plan and a genuine need—keep them, but give them a designated home. For the rest, it's time to move on.

The 'Maybe' Box: A Transitional Strategy for the Hesitant

If the thought of immediately donating or tossing your 'someday' items makes you break into a cold sweat, I have a middle-ground solution: the 'Maybe' Box. This is a single box (or a designated shelf) where you place all the items you're not ready to part with. The key is that the box has a limit—say, one cubic foot or a specific container. You can't keep adding to it indefinitely.

Here's the process: after your audit, put all the 'maybe' items into this box. Seal it with tape and write today's date on it. Then, put the box in a storage area—your garage, attic, or a closet you rarely open. Set a reminder for six months from now. When that date arrives, you have two options: open the box and see if you've needed any of the items. If you haven't touched the box in six months, you can safely donate or sell the entire contents without opening it. If you do open it, you'll likely find that you've forgotten what's inside, which proves you don't need it.

The 'Maybe' Box works because it gives you permission to let go gradually. It removes the pressure of making a permanent decision today. You're simply postponing the decision, but with a clear deadline. This is especially effective for sentimental items or things you've held onto for years. After six months, the emotional attachment often fades, and you can make a more rational choice.

From 'Someday' to 'Today': Turning Aspirations into Action

One of the reasons we keep 'someday' items is that we have vague aspirations but no concrete plan. The bread maker isn't just a kitchen appliance—it's a symbol of your desire to bake fresh bread. The art supplies represent your creative side. The exercise bike is your commitment to health. Instead of letting these items sit unused, you can either commit to them or let them go.

For the items you truly want to use, create a 'Someday' List—a written list of the activities or skills you want to pursue. For each item, write down a specific action plan. For example, if you want to use the bread maker, schedule a Saturday morning to make your first loaf. If you want to paint, sign up for a local class or set aside an hour each week. If you can't commit to a specific time within the next three months, then the item is not a priority. In that case, it's better to donate it and free up space for the things that are actually important to you.

This approach transforms your 'someday' pile from a source of guilt into a catalyst for action. You're either using the items or releasing them. There's no middle ground. By making a conscious choice, you reclaim your agency and your space.

Maintaining a 'Someday-Free' Home: Habits to Prevent the Pile from Returning

Once you've decluttered your 'someday' pile, you need to keep it from coming back. The key is to change your relationship with new purchases and gifts. Before you bring anything into your home, ask yourself: 'Will I use this in the next six months?' If the answer is no, don't buy it. For gifts, it's okay to politely decline or to donate them if they don't serve you. You're not obligated to keep something just because someone gave it to you.

Another habit is to adopt a 'one in, one out' rule for categories that tend to accumulate 'someday' items—like kitchen gadgets, craft supplies, or exercise equipment. When you buy a new item, you must remove an old one. This forces you to be intentional about what you own.

Finally, schedule a quarterly 'someday' check. Set a reminder on your calendar to review any items that might be creeping into the 'someday' category. Ask yourself the audit questions again. This regular maintenance prevents the pile from growing and keeps your home feeling light and open.

Conclusion

The 'someday' pile is a universal challenge, but it doesn't have to control your home or your mind. By understanding why you keep these items, recognizing the hidden costs, and using the 'Someday' Audit and the 'Maybe' Box, you can declutter with compassion and clarity. You'll free up physical space, reduce mental stress, and even save money. More importantly, you'll learn to distinguish between genuine aspirations and vague what-ifs. You'll stop waiting for 'someday' and start living in the present. So go ahead—open that closet, tackle that pile, and give yourself the gift of a home that reflects who you are today, not who you might be someday. Your future self will thank you.
